We consider the general optimization problem

min𝜃∈Θ𝒥︀(𝜃),

(1)

where Θ is a vector space and 𝒥︀ is differentiable. First-order methods solve it iteratively: starting from 𝜃0, they take steps 𝜃𝑡+1=𝜃𝑡+ℎ𝑡, where each step ℎ𝑡 is computed from the derivative 𝐷𝒥︀(𝜃𝑡), and possibly from earlier ones. This post is about how to choose ℎ𝑡, and in particular how the choice of a norm on Θ affects it.

1. Derivatives and gradients

The derivative 𝐷𝒥︀(𝜃) maps a direction ℎ∈Θ to a number, so it is an element of the dual space Θ∗ of linear maps Θ→ℝ, and we write its value at ℎ as the pairing ⟨𝐷𝒥︀(𝜃),ℎ⟩. Both Θ∗ and the pairing come with Θ itself, so nothing is chosen here. An inner product ⟨⋅,⋅⟩Θ on Θ, on the other hand, is a choice, for example the Frobenius inner product. With an inner product chosen, the linearization reads

𝒥︀(𝜃+ℎ)≈𝒥︀(𝜃)+⟨𝐷𝒥︀(𝜃),ℎ⟩=𝒥︀(𝜃)+⟨∇𝜃𝒥︀(𝜃),ℎ⟩Θ.

(2)

The second form holds because every linear map Θ→ℝ is ℎ↦⟨𝑘,ℎ⟩Θ for exactly one 𝑘∈Θ, its Riesz representative,11 This is the Riesz representation theorem, as in Batched Backpropagation as a Linear Solve: 𝑘↦⟨𝑘,⋅⟩Θ is linear and one-to-one, and Θ and Θ∗ have the same dimension. and the gradient ∇𝜃𝒥︀(𝜃) is the representative of 𝐷𝒥︀(𝜃). Unlike the derivative, the gradient lies in Θ, but it depends on the inner product: another inner product gives another gradient of the same function. Only the gradient needs an inner product. The derivative and the steps below need none.

2. Two surrogates

The linearization 𝒥︀(𝜃)+⟨𝑔,ℎ⟩, with 𝑔=𝐷𝒥︀(𝜃), is a good model of 𝒥︀(𝜃+ℎ) only for small ℎ, and on its own it has no minimizer: unless 𝑔=0, it decreases without bound along any direction with ⟨𝑔,ℎ⟩<0. A step therefore needs a second ingredient that says how large ℎ we allow. The length of ℎ∈Θ is measured by a norm ‖⋅‖, which is something that we again have to choose. There are two natural ways to combine the norm with the linearization: as a constraint, or as a penalty,33 The norm here can be any norm on Θ, not just the one induced by the inner product on Θ.

ℎ𝐶=arg min‖ℎ‖≤𝜂⟨𝑔,ℎ⟩,ℎ𝑃=arg minℎ∈Θ⟨𝑔,ℎ⟩+𝜆2‖ℎ‖2.

(4)

The first is a trust-region model: it trusts the linearization only within a radius 𝜂, which caps the length of the step. The second instead penalizes long steps, and as the penalty grows faster than the linearization can decrease, it always has a minimum.44 If 𝒥︀ is 𝐿-smooth with respect to ‖⋅‖, meaning that 𝒥︀(𝜃+ℎ)≤𝒥︀(𝜃)+⟨𝑔,ℎ⟩+𝐿2‖ℎ‖2 for all ℎ, then for 𝜆≥𝐿 the second surrogate is an upper bound on 𝒥︀, and ℎ𝑃 decreases 𝒥︀ by at least the decrease of the surrogate. Convergence analyses use this; in practice 𝜆 is often chosen smaller.

3. The dual norm and the sharp operator

To write the solutions of the constrained and penalized problems in (4) explicitly, we need two things: the dual norm, which measures the size of a derivative in Θ∗, and the sharp operator, which maps a derivative back to an element of Θ. In both cases it is important to note that they depend on the choice of a norm on Θ, rather than the inner product on Θ.

The sharp operator is the map from Θ∗ back to Θ that a norm provides, while the Riesz map is the one that an inner product provides.66 The two coincide exactly when the norm comes from the inner product, ‖ℎ‖2=⟨ℎ,ℎ⟩Θ. The naming convention and notation 𝑔♯ follow Kelner et al. [1].77 Bernstein and Newhouse [2] call 𝑔♯/‖𝑔‖∗ the duality map. Both the constrained and the penalized steps can be written explicitly using the sharp operator. The constrained problem is bounded below by ⟨𝑔,ℎ⟩≥−‖𝑔‖∗‖ℎ‖≥−𝜂‖𝑔‖∗,88 The first inequality is the bound |⟨𝑔,ℎ⟩|≤‖𝑔‖∗‖ℎ‖ from the definition of the dual norm. The second holds because the constraint gives ‖ℎ‖≤𝜂 and ‖𝑔‖∗≥0. which the step below attains, and writing ℎ=𝑘/𝜆 turns the penalized problem into the definition. For 𝑔≠0,

ℎ𝐶=arg min‖ℎ‖≤𝜂⟨𝑔,ℎ⟩=−𝜂𝑔♯‖𝑔‖∗,ℎ𝑃=1𝜆arg min𝑘∈Θ⟨𝑔,𝑘⟩+12‖𝑘‖2=−1𝜆𝑔♯.

(8)

Here 𝑔♯/‖𝑔‖∗ is the unit vector, in the chosen norm, along 𝑔♯, so ℎ𝐶 goes straight to the border of the trust region. Both steps point along −𝑔♯, which depends on the chosen norm, and differ only in length: 𝜂 for ℎ𝐶 and ‖𝑔‖∗/𝜆 for ℎ𝑃, so they coincide for 𝜆=‖𝑔‖∗/𝜂.

4. The effect of the chosen norm

Once the norm is chosen, the step is fixed. To write the steps down, we need coordinates. Let Θ=ℝ𝑚×𝑛, so that 𝜃 is a matrix, for example the weights of a layer in a neural network. The derivative is then represented by the matrix 𝐺 of partial derivatives 𝜕𝒥︀/𝜕𝜃𝑎𝑏, with

⟨𝑔,ℎ⟩=⟨𝐺,ℎ⟩𝐹=tr(𝐺⊤ℎ),𝑔=𝐷𝒥︀(𝜃).

(11)

This uses the Frobenius inner product, which makes 𝐺 the Riesz representative of 𝑔: the gradient for the Frobenius inner product.1010 This might seem like a limitation, but as shown in Section 4.1 every inner product on Θ can be written using the Frobenius inner product. Some norms on Θ, such as the max-norm, only look at the entries of ℎ, as if the matrix were one long vector of 𝑚𝑛 numbers. Others, such as the spectral norm, use that 𝜃 is a matrix, a linear map from inputs to outputs, which is what the weights of a layer are. For matrices, ‖⋅‖1 and ‖⋅‖∞ act on the entries as for vectors, while ‖⋅‖2 is the spectral norm, the largest singular value. Each norm then gives a dual norm and a sharp operator in terms of 𝐺, and with them a known method. Since both the constrained and the penalized steps follow from ‖𝑔‖∗ and 𝑔♯, each example shows only the one that gives the known method. The other step is a rescaled version of it, for example normalized gradient descent in place of gradient descent. The table below collects the norms of this section.1111 The downloadable code computes 𝑔♯ for the Frobenius norm, a diagonal inner product, the max-norm, and the spectral norm by solving the defining problem as a small conic program [3], and prints it next to the closed form.

The norms fall into two families. A norm from an inner product has a linear sharp operator, the Riesz map, and its step is a preconditioned gradient step. Every other norm has a nonlinear sharp operator, and its step is not the gradient for any fixed inner product, one that does not depend on 𝑔.

4.1. Norms from an inner product

Every inner product on Θ can be written as

⟨ℎ,𝑘⟩𝒜︀=⟨𝒜︀(ℎ),𝑘⟩𝐹

(12)

where the linear map 𝒜︀:Θ→Θ is symmetric and positive definite, i.e.,

⟨𝒜︀(ℎ),𝑘⟩𝐹=⟨ℎ,𝒜︀(𝑘)⟩𝐹and⟨𝒜︀(ℎ),ℎ⟩𝐹>0for allℎ≠0.

(13)

Since ⟨𝑔,ℎ⟩=⟨𝐺,ℎ⟩𝐹=⟨𝒜︀−1(𝐺),ℎ⟩𝒜︀,1212 We look for the 𝑘∈Θ with ⟨𝑘,ℎ⟩𝒜︀=⟨𝐺,ℎ⟩𝐹 for all ℎ. By the definition of ⟨⋅,⋅⟩𝒜︀, the left side is ⟨𝒜︀(𝑘),ℎ⟩𝐹, so ⟨𝒜︀(𝑘)−𝐺,ℎ⟩𝐹=0 for all ℎ, which gives 𝒜︀(𝑘)=𝐺 and 𝑘=𝒜︀−1(𝐺). The inverse exists because 𝒜︀ is positive definite. the gradient for ⟨⋅,⋅⟩𝒜︀ is ∇𝒜︀𝒥︀=𝒜︀−1(𝐺).1313 In Example 1, the second inner product is 𝒜︀=diag(1,4), and its gradient (2𝜃1,1/4) is 𝒜︀−1 applied to the first. The inverse 𝒜︀−1 may look unusual, but it is the familiar preconditioner of scientific computing, where a problem is rescaled before it is solved: a gradient step with ∇𝒜︀𝒥︀ is plain gradient descent in the rescaled variables 𝜉=𝒜︀1/2(𝜃).1414 With 𝜉=𝒜︀1/2(𝜃), the derivative of 𝒥︀(𝒜︀−1/2(𝜉)) is represented by 𝒜︀−1/2(𝐺), so a gradient step in 𝜉 changes 𝜉 by −1𝜆𝒜︀−1/2(𝐺), and 𝜃=𝒜︀−1/2(𝜉) by −1𝜆𝒜︀−1(𝐺). With 𝒜︀ close to the curvature of 𝒥︀, the rescaled problem is well conditioned, and the step comes close to Newton’s method.

4.2. Norms not from inner products

Recall that the norm need not come from an inner product. Every method still minimizes the same linearization ⟨𝑔,ℎ⟩, with the derivative 𝑔=𝐷𝒥︀(𝜃)∈Θ∗. What changes is the norm that limits the step, and with it the sharp operator, the map from Θ∗ back to Θ. For a norm that does not come from an inner product, the sharp operator is nonlinear,1515 If 𝑔↦𝑔♯ were linear, then ‖𝑔‖∗2=⟨𝑔,𝑔♯⟩ would be a quadratic form in 𝑔, so the dual norm would come from an inner product on Θ∗, and then so would the norm on Θ, its dual. so it is not the Riesz map of any fixed inner product, and the step is not a gradient for any inner product that does not depend on 𝑔. The max-norm is one such norm: as in the plane, 𝑔♯=‖𝐺‖1sign(𝐺), and its constrained step ℎ𝐶=−𝜂sign(𝐺) is sign descent. The example below shows the spectral norm, whose constrained step is Muon.

5. Incorporating the past

The surrogates above have no memory of the past. The linearization is created using only the current iterate 𝜃𝑡 and its derivative 𝑔𝑡=𝐷𝒥︀(𝜃𝑡), and the norm is fixed. Methods with memory use past iterations in two ways: momentum methods change the linearization, and adaptive methods change the norm.

5.1. Momentum

The idea behind momentum is to build the linearization from past derivatives as well. The surrogates keep their form, with a chosen derivative 𝑔̂𝑡∈Θ∗ in place of 𝑔𝑡,

ℎ𝐶=arg min‖ℎ‖≤𝜂⟨𝑔̂𝑡,ℎ⟩,ℎ𝑃=arg minℎ∈Θ⟨𝑔̂𝑡,ℎ⟩+𝜆2‖ℎ‖2.

(22)

The derivative 𝑔̂𝑡 enters before the sharp operator, in Θ∗. When the sharp operator is linear, as for a norm from an inner product, averaging the derivatives is the same as averaging the steps they give.1717 A linear sharp operator commutes with averaging, (∑𝑘𝑤𝑘𝑔𝑘)♯=∑𝑘𝑤𝑘𝑔𝑘♯. A nonlinear one does not: for Muon, the orthogonalized average of the derivatives is not the average of the orthogonalized derivatives.

5.2. Adaptive norms

Past information can also enter the norm. An adaptive method builds an inner product ⟨⋅,⋅⟩𝒜︀𝑡 from past derivatives, so that the norm changes at every step. Each step is still the one of Example 3, with 𝒜︀𝑡 in place of 𝒜︀,

ℎ𝑃=−1𝜆𝒜︀𝑡−1(𝐺).

(25)

Storing a general 𝒜︀𝑡 is out of the question for a network, so two cheap structures are used: a diagonal one, which stores as many numbers as 𝜃, and a Kronecker-factored one.
