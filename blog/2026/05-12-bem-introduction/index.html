In the previous post the Helmholtz equation was solved using the Finite Element Method (FEM), where the whole domain Ω is divided into elements. The Boundary Element Method (BEM) instead first rewrites the Helmholtz equation as an integral equation that lives only on the boundary Γ=𝜕Ω [1]. As a result, only the boundary has to be divided into elements, which is particularly attractive for exterior problems, such as the sound radiated from a vibrating object into free space, where the domain Ω in theory is infinite.

The building blocks are the same as in the previous post: the unknown functions are approximated as 𝑝(𝐱)≈𝐓(𝐱)𝐩, the elements are parameterized by local coordinates 𝐮∈ℒ︀𝑒, the element localization matrices 𝐋𝑒 pick out the local values 𝐩𝑒=𝐋𝑒𝐩, and integrals over the elements are approximated by a quadrature rule. The only difference in these building blocks is that the elements are now surface elements Γ𝑒 instead of volume elements Ω𝑒. What changes is the equation being discretized and the approach used to discretize it.

1. The Kirchhoff-Helmholtz integral equation

For time-harmonic problems the pressure can be written as 𝑝(𝐱,𝑡)=Re{𝑝(𝐱)𝑒𝛼𝑖𝜔𝑡}, where 𝛼=±1 is the sign of the chosen time dependency. Inserting this into the wave equation results in the Helmholtz equation

Δ𝑝(𝐱)+𝑘2𝑝(𝐱)=0,𝐱∈Ω,

(1)

which looks independent of 𝛼. This can cause confusion when the time dependency is not stated explicitly, as the relation between the normal derivative of the pressure and the normal velocity 𝑣𝐧 does depend on 𝛼

𝜕𝑝𝜕𝐧(𝐱)=𝑠(𝛼)𝑘𝑣𝐧(𝐱),𝑠(𝛼)=−𝛼𝑖𝜌0𝑐0,𝐱∈Γ,

(2)

where the normal 𝐧 points into the domain Ω, i.e. out of a vibrating object, and 𝑠(𝛼) is introduced for simplicity, similarly to [2]. Note that this is opposite to the previous post, where the boundary term of the integration by parts used the outward normal of Ω.

In three dimensions the Green’s function, which will come in handy soon enough, is given by

𝐺(𝐱,𝐲)=exp(−𝛼𝑖𝑘‖𝐱−𝐲‖2)4𝜋‖𝐱−𝐲‖2,𝐱,𝐲∈ℝ3.

(3)

It is the solution of the Helmholtz equation with a point source

Δ𝐺(𝐱,𝐲)+𝑘2𝐺(𝐱,𝐲)=−𝛿(𝐱−𝐲),

(4)

where 𝛿 is the Dirac delta function, while the sign 𝛼 makes the waves of 𝐺 travel outwards from the source. The Green’s function takes two inputs, 𝐱 and 𝐲, commonly referred to as the target location (𝐱) and the source location (𝐲), or simply the target(s) and source(s).

The derivation of the integral equation starts in the same way as the FEM: we multiply the Helmholtz equation with a test function 𝑞 and integrate over the domain

∫Ω𝑞(𝐲)(Δ𝑝(𝐲)+𝑘2𝑝(𝐲))d𝐲=0.

(5)

The difference is that we now integrate by parts twice, moving both derivatives from 𝑝 onto 𝑞. Writing the normal derivative of 𝑝 in terms of the normal velocity, it follows that

∫Ω𝑝(𝐲)(Δ𝑞(𝐲)+𝑘2𝑞(𝐲))d𝐲+∫Γ𝜕𝑞𝜕𝐧(𝐲)𝑝(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲−𝑠(𝛼)𝑘∫Γ𝑞(𝐲)𝑣𝐧(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲=0,

where the signs of the boundary terms are due to 𝐧 pointing into Ω. For exterior problems the boundary also includes a sphere at infinity, whose integral vanishes when 𝑝 satisfies the Sommerfeld radiation condition below. Choosing the test function as the Green’s function, 𝑞(𝐲)=𝐺(𝐱,𝐲), the volume integral collapses due to (4), and we are left with the Kirchhoff-Helmholtz integral equation

𝜁(𝐱)𝑝(𝐱)−∫Γ𝜕𝐺(𝐱,𝐲)𝜕𝐧(𝐲)𝑝(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲+𝑠(𝛼)𝑘∫Γ𝐺(𝐱,𝐲)𝑣𝐧(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲=0,

(6)

where 𝜁(𝐱) is the so-called integral free term depending on the geometry at 𝐱. For 𝐱 in the interior of Ω it is equal to one, in which case (6) gives the pressure at 𝐱 from the values of 𝑝 and 𝑣𝐧 on the boundary. For 𝐱 on a smooth part of the boundary it is equal to 1/2, while at corners and edges it takes values between 0 and 1. Setting 𝐱∈Γ makes (6) an equation involving only functions on the boundary: a boundary integral equation.

The time dependency still enters (6) through 𝐺 and 𝑠(𝛼). Changing 𝛼 conjugates the Green’s function and flips the sign of 𝑠(𝛼), so a sign error in either of them results in a solution of the wrong problem. Finally, the derivation assumes that 𝑝 satisfies the Sommerfeld radiation condition

lim𝑟→∞𝑟(𝑑−1)/2(𝜕𝑝𝜕𝑟+𝛼𝑖𝑘𝑝)=0,

(7)

where 𝑑 is the dimension, meaning that no waves come in from infinity. In the FEM this condition has to be imposed by truncating the domain with an artificial boundary, whereas in the BEM it is built into the Green’s function, so that the solution of (6) satisfies it automatically.

2. Collocation

As in the FEM, we multiply (6) with a test function 𝜑, this time integrating over the boundary,

∫Γ𝜑(𝐱)(𝜁(𝐱)𝑝(𝐱)−∫Γ𝜕𝐺(𝐱,𝐲)𝜕𝐧(𝐲)𝑝(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲+𝑠(𝛼)𝑘∫Γ𝐺(𝐱,𝐲)𝑣𝐧(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲)d𝑆𝐱=0.

(8)

Choosing 𝜑(𝐱)≈𝐚⊤𝐓(𝐱)⊤ would result in a Galerkin BEM. The integrals of the Galerkin BEM are, however, double surface integrals with a singular integrand, which makes them expensive to compute. Instead, the most common approach is the so-called collocation approach, where the test function is a sum of Dirac delta functions placed at the collocation points 𝐭1,…,𝐭𝑛∈Γ

𝜑(𝐱)=𝐚⊤[𝛿(𝐱−𝐭1)𝛿(𝐱−𝐭2)⋮𝛿(𝐱−𝐭𝑛)],

(9)

where 𝐚∈ℂ𝑛 is again a vector of arbitrary coefficients. The collocation points are in most cases chosen as the interpolation nodes of the basis functions, so that the cardinal property 𝐓(𝐭𝑗)𝐩=𝑝𝑗 can be used. Inserting 𝜑 into (8), the sifting property of the Dirac delta function evaluates the outer integral at the collocation points, so that (8) becomes 𝐚⊤𝐫=0 with

𝐫=[𝜁(𝐭1)𝑝(𝐭1)⋮𝜁(𝐭𝑛)𝑝(𝐭𝑛)]−[∫Γ𝜕𝐺(𝐭1,𝐲)𝜕𝐧(𝐲)𝑝(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲⋮∫Γ𝜕𝐺(𝐭𝑛,𝐲)𝜕𝐧(𝐲)𝑝(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲]+𝑠(𝛼)𝑘[∫Γ𝐺(𝐭1,𝐲)𝑣𝐧(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲⋮∫Γ𝐺(𝐭𝑛,𝐲)𝑣𝐧(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲].

In its current form the unknowns are still functions, which a computer cannot be tasked to find. Instead, we insert the approximations 𝑝(𝐲)≈𝐓(𝐲)𝐩 and 𝑣𝐧(𝐲)≈𝐓(𝐲)𝐯𝐧 and handle the three vectors one at a time. For the first vector, the cardinal property of the basis functions gives

[𝜁(𝐭1)𝑝(𝐭1)⋮𝜁(𝐭𝑛)𝑝(𝐭𝑛)]≈[𝜁(𝐭1)𝐓(𝐭1)𝐩⋮𝜁(𝐭𝑛)𝐓(𝐭𝑛)𝐩]=[𝜁(𝐭1)𝑝1⋮𝜁(𝐭𝑛)𝑝𝑛]=diag(𝜻)𝐩,

(10)

where 𝜻=[𝜁(𝐭1)…𝜁(𝐭𝑛)]⊤. For the two integrals, the coefficients 𝐩 and 𝐯𝐧 do not depend on 𝐲 and can be moved outside of the integrals

[∫Γ𝜕𝐺(𝐭1,𝐲)𝜕𝐧(𝐲)𝑝(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲⋮∫Γ𝜕𝐺(𝐭𝑛,𝐲)𝜕𝐧(𝐲)𝑝(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲]≈[∫Γ𝜕𝐺(𝐭1,𝐲)𝜕𝐧(𝐲)𝐓(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲⋮∫Γ𝜕𝐺(𝐭𝑛,𝐲)𝜕𝐧(𝐲)𝐓(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲]⏟𝐅𝐩,

(11)

[∫Γ𝐺(𝐭1,𝐲)𝑣𝐧(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲⋮∫Γ𝐺(𝐭𝑛,𝐲)𝑣𝐧(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲]≈[∫Γ𝐺(𝐭1,𝐲)𝐓(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲⋮∫Γ𝐺(𝐭𝑛,𝐲)𝐓(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲]⏟𝐆𝐯𝐧,

(12)

where each entry of the vectors is a row vector, as 𝐓(𝐲) is, so that 𝐅 and 𝐆 are 𝑛×𝑛 matrices. Collecting the terms, 𝐚⊤𝐫=0 becomes

𝐚⊤((diag(𝜻)−𝐅)𝐩+𝑠(𝛼)𝑘𝐆𝐯𝐧)≈0.

(13)

As this has to hold for all 𝐚∈ℂ𝑛, the term inside the parentheses must vanish, and the collocation BEM therefore reads

(diag(𝜻)−𝐅)𝐩+𝑠(𝛼)𝑘𝐆𝐯𝐧=𝐇𝐩+𝑠(𝛼)𝑘𝐆𝐯𝐧≈𝟎,

(14)

where we simply defined 𝐇=diag(𝜻)−𝐅 for the sake of convenience. In a typical problem 𝐯𝐧 is known (e.g. from the vibration of a structure) and (14) is solved for 𝐩.

3. Computing the matrices

While (14) is easily stated, it does not mention how to actually compute 𝐅 and 𝐆. The procedure is the same as for the mass and stiffness matrices in the previous post: the boundary integral is split into a sum over the elements, each element integral is moved to local coordinates, and the result is approximated by a quadrature rule. On element 𝑒 the pressure is interpolated as 𝑝(𝐲𝑒(𝐮))≈𝐓𝑒(𝐮)𝐋𝑒𝐩, so that the 𝑘th row of 𝐅 becomes

∫Γ𝜕𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲)𝜕𝐧(𝐲)𝐓(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲≈∑𝑒=1𝑁(∫ℒ︀𝑒𝜕𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲𝑒(𝐮))𝜕𝐧(𝐲𝑒(𝐮))𝐓𝑒(𝐮)jacobian(Γ𝑒,𝐮)d𝐮)𝐋𝑒≈∑𝑒=1𝑁(∑𝑖=1𝑄(𝐭𝑘,𝑒)𝜕𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲𝑒(𝐮𝑖))𝜕𝐧(𝐲𝑒(𝐮𝑖))𝐓𝑒(𝐮𝑖)jacobian(Γ𝑒,𝐮𝑖)𝑤𝑖)𝐋𝑒,

(15)

where the first approximation is due to the geometry being approximated by the elements, and the second is due to the quadrature. The 𝑘th row of 𝐆 is computed in the same way, with 𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲) in place of its normal derivative. The jacobian describes the local deformation of the map from local to global coordinates. For the curve elements of 2D problems this is a change of length, while for the surface elements of 3D problems it is a change of area

2D:jacobian(Γ𝑒,𝑢)=‖𝐗𝑒d𝐍𝑒(𝑢)d𝑢‖2(length deformation),3D:jacobian(Γ𝑒,𝐮)=‖(𝐗𝑒𝜕𝐍𝑒(𝐮)𝜕𝑢1)×(𝐗𝑒𝜕𝐍𝑒(𝐮)𝜕𝑢2)‖2(area deformation).

(16)

The new difficulty compared to the FEM is that the Green’s function is singular at 𝐲=𝐭𝑘, so a standard quadrature rule is inaccurate on the elements that contain, or lie close to, the collocation point. This is why (15) highlights that the number of quadrature points 𝑄(𝐭𝑘,𝑒) can depend on both the collocation point and the element. For the elements containing 𝐭𝑘, special techniques are needed, such as splitting the element at 𝐭𝑘 and using a transformation whose jacobian cancels the 1/𝑟 singularity.

4. Acceleration methods

A considerable downside of the BEM is that 𝐇,𝐆∈ℂ𝑛×𝑛 are dense, unlike the sparse mass and stiffness matrices of the FEM. The memory used to store them therefore scales as 𝒪︀(𝑛2), and a direct solve of (14) is 𝒪︀(𝑛3), rendering the direct application of the method unusable for large 𝑛. This is somewhat offset by the fact that only the boundary is discretized, but for large problems it is a serious limitation. However, various methods have been developed that reduce the memory and the cost of a matrix-vector product to 𝒪︀(𝑛log𝑛) or even 𝒪︀(𝑛) by exploiting the rank structure of the problem. As these methods only define the matrix-vector products, (14) must then be solved by an iterative method, the most common being the generalized minimal residual method (GMRES) [3], [4], [5].

The aim of this section is to show how the matrix-vector products with 𝐆 and 𝐅 can be computed in 𝒪︀(𝑛) (or 𝒪︀(𝑛log𝑛)) time using the fast multipole method (FMM) or hierarchical matrices (ℋ︀-matrices). To do so, we start by assuming that all elements are integrated using the same 𝑄 quadrature points.11 This results in inaccurate integrals for the elements close to the collocation point, which are fixed by a near-field correction below. Multiplying the 𝑘th row of 𝐆 with a vector 𝐳 it follows that

(∫Γ𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲)𝐓(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲)𝐳≈(∑𝑒=1𝑁∑𝑖=1𝑄𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲𝑒(𝐮𝑖))jacobian(Γ𝑒,𝐮𝑖)𝑤𝑖𝐓𝑒(𝐮𝑖)𝐋𝑒)𝐳=(∑𝑗=1𝑁𝑄𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲𝑗)jacobian(Γ𝑒(𝑗),𝐮𝑗)𝑤𝑗𝐓𝑒(𝑗)(𝐮𝑗)𝐋𝑒(𝑗)⏟𝑗th row of𝐂)𝐳=[𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲1)𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲2)…𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲𝑁𝑄)]𝐂𝐳,

(18)

where the subscript 𝑗 refers to an ordering of the quadrature points of all elements, and 𝑒(𝑗) returns the element that quadrature point 𝑗 lies on. The matrix 𝐂∈ℝ𝑁𝑄×𝑛 is extremely sparse and can be thought of as a map from the values 𝐳 at the collocation points to the coefficients 𝐜=𝐂𝐳 at the quadrature points 𝐲𝑗. The same approach applies to 𝐅, where the normal derivative is written as 𝐧(𝐲𝑗)⊤∇𝐲𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲𝑗)

(∫Γ𝜕𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲)𝜕𝐧(𝐲)𝐓(𝐲)d𝑆𝐲)𝐳≈[𝐧(𝐲1)⊤∇𝐲𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲1)…𝐧(𝐲𝑁𝑄)⊤∇𝐲𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲𝑁𝑄)]𝐂𝐳.

(19)

Applying (18) or (19) directly for all 𝑛 rows results in products that scale as 𝒪︀(𝑁𝑄𝑛), which could hurt more than we gain, as 𝑁𝑄≳𝑛. The point is that the dense part of the product, a sum of Green’s functions over the points 𝐲𝑗, is exactly what the FMM and ℋ︀-matrices accelerate. Doing so blindly, however, keeps the inaccurate integration of the elements close to the collocation points. To fix this, a sparse near-field correction 𝐒 is added, which subtracts the inaccurate integration using only 𝑄 quadrature points and adds the accurate one. Applying (18) or (19) for all rows at the same time can therefore be thought of as

𝐀=𝐁𝐂+𝐒,𝐀,𝐒∈ℂ𝑛×𝑛,𝐁∈ℂ𝑛×𝑁𝑄,𝐂∈ℝ𝑁𝑄×𝑛,

(20)

where 𝐁 is the dense part approximated by either the FMM or an ℋ︀-matrix (see (22) and (23) below), 𝐂 is the coefficient map, and 𝐒 is the near-field correction. As 𝐒 and 𝐂 are highly sparse, both their assembly and their products scale as 𝒪︀(𝑛𝜏) with 𝜏≪𝑛. Using an approximation of 𝐁 whose product scales linearly in time and memory therefore results in a representation of 𝐀 that scales similarly.

To compute 𝐒 efficiently, its sparsity pattern must be known in advance. For collocation this could, for example, be the elements neighboring each interpolation node, as shown in Figure 2. For discontinuous elements, whose nodes lie inside the elements, the support of each basis function is a single element (panel (b)). While this works well in many cases, it fails when elements that are not neighbors are geometrically close, as is the case for e.g. thin structures.

4.1. The fast multipole method

The FMM can be used to accelerate sums of the Green’s function (𝐆) as well as of its normal derivative (𝐅) [6], [7], [8]. Throughout the years, many good resources explaining the intricacies of the FMM have been written. For a gentle introduction I recommend the short course by Greengard and the pedestrian introduction by Ying [9], while the books by Gumerov and Duraiswami [10] and Liu [11] give a more detailed look with an emphasis on the BEM. Ying also has a wonderful introductory talk. Given these resources, the details of the FMM are left out here, and we focus on the connection to the BEM. For this, it is enough to think of the FMM as a way of speeding up sums of the form

𝑢(𝐭𝑘)=∑𝑗=1𝑁𝑄𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲𝑗)𝑐𝑗,𝑢(𝐭𝑘)=∑𝑗=1𝑁𝑄𝐧(𝐲𝑗)⊤∇𝐲𝐺(𝐭𝑘,𝐲𝑗)𝑐𝑗,

(21)

where the 𝑗th term is left out when 𝐭𝑘=𝐲𝑗. Looking back at (18) and (19), this is exactly what is computed for each row of 𝐆 and 𝐅, with the coefficients 𝐜=𝐂𝐳. For a fixed precision, applying the FMM to all 𝑛 collocation points scales as 𝒪︀(𝑛+𝑁𝑄) at low frequencies, and as 𝒪︀((𝑛+𝑁𝑄)log(𝑛+𝑁𝑄)) for the wideband methods needed at high frequencies. Many FMM libraries exist, such as ExaFMM, ScalFMM and FMM3D, the last of which has a Julia interface. For 2D problems, the companion library FMM2D can be used from Julia through FMM2D.jl.22 I maintain this Julia package.

4.2. Hierarchical matrices

The ℋ︀-matrix approach differs from the FMM in that it does not accelerate the sums directly [12], [13]. Instead, it approximates the matrix that corresponds to computing the sums for all 𝑛 collocation points at the same time. For 𝐆 this is the matrix

𝐁=[𝐺(𝐭1,𝐲1)𝐺(𝐭1,𝐲2)…𝐺(𝐭1,𝐲𝑁𝑄)𝐺(𝐭2,𝐲1)𝐺(𝐭2,𝐲2)…𝐺(𝐭2,𝐲𝑁𝑄)⋮⋮⋱⋮𝐺(𝐭𝑛,𝐲1)𝐺(𝐭𝑛,𝐲2)…𝐺(𝐭𝑛,𝐲𝑁𝑄)],

(22)

where the approximation is based on the fact that subblocks of the matrix corresponding to points far from each other are well approximated by low-rank matrices. For 𝐅 the matrix is

𝐁=[𝐧(𝐲1)⊤∇𝐲𝐺(𝐭1,𝐲1)…𝐧(𝐲𝑁𝑄)⊤∇𝐲𝐺(𝐭1,𝐲𝑁𝑄)⋮⋱⋮𝐧(𝐲1)⊤∇𝐲𝐺(𝐭𝑛,𝐲1)…𝐧(𝐲𝑁𝑄)⊤∇𝐲𝐺(𝐭𝑛,𝐲𝑁𝑄)].

(23)

How the hierarchical low-rank approximation is computed is outside the scope of this post, but the interested reader can look at [14], [15], [16], of which [14] is my preferred introduction. Furthermore, a wonderful lecture series by Alex Barnett, Adrianna Gillman and Per-Gunnar Martinsson from the 2014 CBMS-NSF Conference: Fast Direct Solvers for Elliptic PDEs can be found online. There are many implementations of hierarchical matrices, such as HLIBpro, HLib and H2Lib, and in Julia KernelMatrices.jl [17], HssMatrices.jl and HMatrices.jl implement various formats with different properties. A longer list can be found here. An often mentioned strength of ℋ︀-matrices is that their LU factorization can be computed efficiently, which is useful when iterative solvers converge slowly, e.g. near resonances, although the ranks, and thereby the cost, grow with the frequency. However, an LU factorization cannot be applied directly to the form of (20).

4.3. The interpolated factored Green function

A more recent approach is the interpolated factored Green function (IFGF) method [18]. An advantage of the IFGF is that it does not need the split into low and high frequencies required by the FMM, while at the same time being easily parallelized, as it does not rely on the fast Fourier transform [19]. However, as opposed to the purely algebraic ℋ︀-matrices, the IFGF is based on interpolation. It therefore requires that the matrix is generated by a function that can be evaluated at any pair of points (𝐱,𝐲). Unfortunately, this is not the case for 𝐅, as its entries depend on the normal, which is computed from the local coordinates 𝐮 of the elements. A workaround is to approximate the full gradient of the Green’s function with the IFGF, as it can be evaluated at any (𝐱,𝐲), and then compute the normal derivative from it. A simple implementation of the method can be found in IFGF.jl.
